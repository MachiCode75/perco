# 06 — Storytelling engine (from the 6 transcribed references)

All six stories run on the same engine. Use it to adapt any user story (keep the user's plot; rebuild
the telling). Short quotes below are analysis examples — never reuse a reference plot or its lines.

## 1. The opening line (6/6)
**"In this town / In my town, <impossible rule stated as a boring fact>."** + one concrete consequence.
- "…when you fall asleep you float up into the sky and you don't come down until the sun comes up."
- "…the monsters eat everyone. Except me."
- "…nobody is allowed to blink."
The rule is supernatural, simple, visual, and has an obvious daily-life cost. No intro, no "hi guys".

## 2. The town copes (first 15–25 %) — charm + believability
Three to five **mundane adaptations**, each with a concrete number or a funny detail:
- rituals: boards nailed at the church bell, ropes tied to bedposts, clips holding eyelids, paying to hold
  the hero's backpack ($40), a waiting list, "31 weddings, 200 first dates";
- one **comic townsperson** with a single quirk (the mayor sleeps on 11 ropes because he is scared of
  heights; Mr Lund snores so hard he spins);
- a **local saying / rule everybody repeats** ("Nobody here looks up", "Never take the old path").
Tone: deadpan kid voice, amused, cozy. This is what makes the viewer like the world before it breaks.

## 3. Attachment (before anything bad happens)
The viewer must love 1–2 characters **through small acts, not adjectives**:
- a **vulnerable little sibling** with one wish/question ("every day she asks me what grass feels like");
- a **pet or old helper** dismissed as useless ("the most useless dog in town");
- a **best friend** with a different view of the threat ("it doesn't look hungry, it looks sad");
- a **parent's ritual** (the same lullaby every night, checking the knot, checking the boards twice);
- a **micro-story** with a bittersweet sting ("…he waved back with both hands. She was still waving
  when it was over.").
Give the hero a **personal stake/status**: the exception ("except me"), new independence ("I'm nine, so I
tie my own knot now"), the newcomer, or a missing memory.

## 4. Seeds = clues hidden in plain sight (3–5)
Odd details the hero notices and the viewer files away. Each one is **explained by the twist**:
teeth marks on the rope · bandaged fingers + scratches at an adult's height + "it was the dog — we don't
have a dog" · the monsters never try a door, they step aside, they nod · a plaque "the one who never
slept" · faces going blurry, a beeping only he hears. Standard pivot line: **"Then I started noticing
things."** One clue can come from a **document** (a notebook with three cryptic lines → text card).

## 5. The taboo is broken — the hero acts
Escalation (numbers shrinking/growing, things disappearing) forces the hero to **break the town rule**
(pull a nail out, walk the old path, take the sister outside, lead the town out, close the eyes). Climax
is physical and fast: a chase, a fall, a rope going tight, light flooding in. Onomatopoeia as beats:
**"Tug. Tug. Tug."**, "Crash. Then silence.", "Blink." — each is its own shot.

## 6. The twist (≈ 60–75 %) — inversion
Formula, almost word for word in every story:
**"The <feared thing> was never <what we believed>. It was <the opposite>."** then 2–4 lines of
**"That's why …"** paying off the seeds.
Types seen: protector ↔ threat swap (the moon was saving the kids from the parents; the dog was catching
the hero every night) · the hero was the cause ("I wasn't special. I was the door.") · reality reveal
(coma dream; the beeping was the heart monitor) · identity reveal (the creature bows: "Master").

## 7. The ending — emotional payoff + sting
- Pay off the attachment character (sibling, pet, friend): sacrifice, rescue, or loss.
- **Last line = callback to an early line, completed or inverted**: "Nobody in this town looks up. He
  does." · "Don't let go." / "I won't." → her hands on the backpack get lighter · "…her window was still
  boarded shut." (dread cliffhanger on the loved character).
- Short final sentence, concrete image, no moral, no explanation after the sting.

## 8. Language mechanics
- First person kid (9–12), past tense for events, present for the world's rules and the final state.
- Numbers everywhere (ages, counts, money, years, times like 3:00).
- Very short dialogue, ≤ 5 lines, iconic and often **repeated** later ("Don't let go." / "I won't.").
- Run-on "and … and …" sentences for action, 1–3-word punches after them.
- Plain vocabulary, one or two similes max ("like two hundred kites").

## 9. Adaptation template (fill before writing the screenplay)
```
RULE (hook, ≤15 words):
COPING ×3 (number/joke each):           COMIC TOWNSPERSON:            LOCAL SAYING (refrain):
HERO (age, status/stake):               LOVED ONE(S) + small act of love:
TABOO:                                  SEEDS ×3–5 (each → explained later):
ESCALATION (countdown/numbers):         HERO BREAKS TABOO by:
TWIST "X was never Y. It was Z." + "That's why…" ×2–3:
PAYOFF (loved one):                     FINAL LINE (callback, inverted/completed):
```
Budget for ~160 s: hook 5 % · coping 15 % · attachment 10 % · seeds 15 % · escalation 15 % ·
climax 10 % · twist 12 % · payoff + sting 18 %.
