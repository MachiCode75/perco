# 03 — Writing rules (story → narration in the reference style)

Use these rules when turning ANY user story into the `say` text of an episode. They describe
the reference's construction. Do not reuse its plot or wording; only its form and devices.

## 1. Targets (measured on the reference)

| metric | reference | rule |
|---|---|---|
| runtime | 170.7 s | 140–180 s |
| words | 757 | **620–780 words** at the reference pace (4.4 words/s). If the TTS is slower, either raise the voice speed (≈ 1.25–1.35×) or cut words: **words ≈ target seconds × measured TTS words/s**. Never go over 180 s |
| speech rate | 266 wpm, pauses 0.12–0.25 s, ≈ 3 s silence in total | dense, no long pauses; do not write "…" or dramatic silences |
| sentences | 97, mean 7.8 words, median 7 | 80–110 sentences |
| sentence length | p10 2, p25 4, p75 11, p90 16, max 24 | **half of the sentences ≤ 6 words, ~20 % ≤ 3 words**, ≤ 15 % over 15 words, none over 25 |
| paragraphs / beats | ~14 beats | see §3 |

## 2. Voice
- **First person, a kid or young teen narrator** ("my little brother", "Mom", "Grandpa"). The
  narrator is calm, matter-of-fact and slightly deadpan. They report strange things as normal
  town life. Plain spoken vocabulary ("dumb stuff", "did the worm"), no literary adjectives, no
  metaphors except one or two simple similes ("Like an echo.", "Like a mirror.").
- **Tense plan:** present tense while describing how the world works (the first ~33 % of the
  runtime: "Every morning half the town sits…"). Switch to **past tense at the first personal
  incident** ("One night I turned off my bedroom light…") and stay there. The rule statement
  comes back in the present at the end, inverted.
- The narrator rarely names feelings. Show them through actions or other people ("Grandpa didn't
  sleep.", "He was crying."). One line of self-doubt is enough ("Told myself I imagined it.").
- Address: no "you" to the viewer, except the generic "you'd" for town habits ("You'd drop a
  plate and…"), which drives the escalation montage.
- Elliptic subjects are fine and typical ("Told myself I imagined it.", "Looked at the lake for
  a long time.").

## 3. Structure: beats with reference timestamps (scale them to your runtime)

| # | beat | ref. time | % of run | what it does |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Hook = the rule of the world** | 0–4.4 s | 0–3 % | 2–3 short sentences: a place + an impossible rule stated flatly. The first words are on screen at 0.1 s. |
| 2 | Rule triplet | 4.4–6.6 | 3–4 % | three 2-word fragments ("Same X. Same Y. Same Z.") |
| 3 | Town ritual + 3 funny examples | 6.6–22.4 | 4–13 % | how the community lives with the rule; each example comes in two halves (us / them); light, cozy, humorous |
| 4 | **Personal example + recurring object** | 22.4–30.4 | 13–18 % | family member (little brother) + an object/action that will return (a hand-made sign with a 2-word text, a silly dance) |
| 5 | **First wrong detail** | 30.4–37.5 | 18–22 % | "There's only one thing off about them." plus one concrete creepy image (faces always turned toward us) |
| 6 | **Elder lore + countdown device** | 37.5–56.6 | 22–33 % | the elder's saying (quoted, becomes the refrain), history of the gap shrinking (year → week → day), the elder's measuring object (stopwatch), numbers ("23 hours. 22 hours."), seasons passing |
| 7 | Escalation, personal | 56.6–76.7 | 33–45 % | first incident in the past tense (the other side is *ahead*), then a montage of the gap shrinking: minutes → seconds → "like an echo" |
| 8 | Town reaction | 76.7–83.7 | 45–49 % | authority figure's plan, collective action (nobody moves for 3 days, curtains shut) |
| 9 | Elder prophecy | 83.7–92.1 | 49–54 % | night scene, narrator asks, elder gives one ominous quoted line, refuses the follow-up ("He didn't answer.") |
| 10 | **Climax** | 92.1–103 | 54–60 % | the measuring object goes silent ("nothing left to time"), perfect mirror moment |
| 11 | **Twist** | 103–118 | 60–69 % | a test fails. The other side did it first. Reframe the whole premise in 2–3 short lines ("They were never X. They were Y."), then state the new rule |
| 12 | Emotional beat + recurring object | 118–133.7 | 69–78 % | parent's protective gesture and a quoted comfort line, already said on the other side. The recurring object comes back changed ("hi ME" → "bye ME") |
| 13 | The other side's end | 133.7–145 | 78–85 % | the other side does something final and silent (walks into the lake, holding hands) → "And nobody came back up. Not one of them." → stillness |
| 14 | **Dark accelerating ending** | 145–167 | 85–98 % | it now happens to us: triplet of named people, the parent lets go, the refrain repeated and inverted ("Not anymore. Now whatever they do, we do."), body-level countdown ("my knees. My chest. My chin.") |
| 15 | **Final image = the recurring object** | 167–170.7 | 98–100 % | one sentence describing the object left behind, ending on its text/name. Last words = the payoff. No moral, no explanation |

Ratio rules: the comedic, cozy part fills the **first 22 %**. Dread builds until **60 %**. The
twist lands at **≈ 60–69 %**. The last **22 %** is the tragic ending. The ending is not slower:
keep the same sentence density.

## 4. Devices to use (with reference counts)
- **Refrain / rule sentence** spoken 3 times: hook (paraphrased), the elder's quote (22 %), and
  the inverted ending (91 %). The two quoted versions are captioned in the elder's colour.
- **Copy naming**: the double of a character is "Other" + name ("Other Toby", "Other Me's
  light"). It is used 15 times. Invent an equally short, capitalised tag for your story's
  doubles or opposites.
- **Pivot phrase** that sends the viewer to the other side: "across the lake" ×8, "across the
  water" ×3. Each one is a natural cut point to a binocular or far-shore shot.
- **Countdown with concrete numbers**: year → week → one day; 23 hours, 22 hours; minutes →
  seconds; "for 3 days"; "on the third night"; "a second later" ×2. Write numbers as digits
  (they are captioned that way).
- **Triplets**: "Same houses. Same streets. Same people." / "Mr Lee. The mayor. Grandpa." /
  "my knees. My chest. My chin."
- **Mirrored pairs**: an event on our side, then the same on theirs, in one or two sentences.
  The reel uses this for every example.
- **Recurring objects** (callbacks): a sign with 2-word text (hi ME → bye ME → floating sign at
  the end), binoculars (raised, then abandoned), stopwatch (pressed, then not pressed), a
  silly action (the worm: eaten → copied → done first).
- **Quoted speech**: max 4–5 short quotes in the whole story (2–6 words each). Put the tag after
  the quote ("he whispered", "Grandpa always says"). One speaker colour per character in captions.
- **Short punchy fragments** after a long sentence ("Like an echo." "Not one of them."
  "Slowly. Holding hands.").
- **Seasons and time markers** to show time passing: "By winter…", "By spring…", "A few weeks
  later…", "On the third night…", "The morning…".
- **Hard sentence-initial connectors** that open shots: "Then", "And", "But", "So", "Now".

## 5. Writing for the shot list (so it can be illustrated)
- Every sentence must be **picturable** with the engine: a named character doing one visible
  action in a known set (dock, street, house front, kitchen, living room, bedroom, shore/water,
  church, park). About 85 % of phrases are shown literally. Give each phrase a concrete noun or
  verb that can be shown.
- Put a **visible noun or action at the end of each clause**, because the cut lands on the
  payoff word ("…holds up a sign that says | hi ME").
- Keep the cast small: narrator + 1 sibling + 1 parent + 1 elder + 2–3 named townspeople + an
  authority figure + anonymous crowd. Give each a visual trademark (hair colour, striped shirt,
  glasses + vest, suit + chain).
- Plan 3–6 **"look" moments** (someone raises binoculars, looks out of a window, looks across
  the water). They justify the binocular POV shots (≈ 15 % of runtime).
- Include at least one night sequence, one interior sequence and one season change, to vary the
  look (the reference spends 11 % of its time at night, 11 % indoors, 2 % in snow).

## 6. What to avoid
- No narrator explanations of mechanics, no lore dumps over 2 sentences, no meta talk ("you
  won't believe…", "part 2", "follow for more").
- No long dialogue scenes. No more than 2 consecutive sentences of dialogue.
- No ambiguous pronoun chains across many sentences. Repeat names ("Grandpa", "Toby").
- No actions the engine cannot show (vehicles chasing, fine hand manipulation, fire, gore,
  complex choreography), and no large city settings.
- No happy-ending summary or moral. End on the image.
- No punctuation-dependent effects (ellipses, ALL CAPS shouting) except a 2-word sign text in
  caps.
- Do not exceed 25 words in one sentence. Do not let 3 sentences in a row exceed 12 words.

## 7. Checklist before writing `episode.json`
1. Word count fits the target duration at the measured TTS rate.
2. The hook states the rule in ≤ 15 words within the first 2 sentences.
3. Beats 4, 5, 6, 10, 11, 12, 15 are all present (object, wrong detail, elder + countdown,
   climax, twist, emotional callback, final object image).
4. The refrain appears 3×. The recurring object appears ≥ 3× and closes the story.
5. ≥ 45 % of sentences are ≤ 6 words. At least 2 triplets. At least 3 numbers.
6. Quotes are short, tagged after, and listed for coloured captions.
